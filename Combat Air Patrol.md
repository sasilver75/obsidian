[[Defensive Counter-Air]] (DCA): We mean a defensive line of airborne guards defending the rest of our forces; could mean escorting high-value assets like tankers or [[E-3 Sentry|AWACS]] or being an umbrella over troops/civilians on the ground, or being a line of protection on a border.

The CAP can hang out in orbit near a fixed point in the air, or it can be ground-alert fighters sitting on an airfield for a call to intercept a target (saves fuel, worse response time). An airborne CAP will always respond faster than a ground-alert fighter. Typically continuously manned by a flight of fighters in 3 hour shifts. USAF requires a 12-hour rest opportunity between flight duty periods with a maximum of 12 hours in each flight duty period... this means that you might need 32 pilots and their planes to cover a 4-aircraft CAP station for 24 hours a day. 

The time period that a CAP patrol is expected to be on station is known as the ==Vulnerability Window==, or "Vul" for short; this is the time on-station; planning, debriefing, and transit to station don't count. 

You might see an arrangement where several CAP stations are protecting a broad front, with each flight is responsible for its own area, known as a ==Lane==. This also requires some coordination; you can expect to see some sort of airborne controller in an [[E-3 Sentry|AWACS]] aircraft or a ground-based [[Control and Reporting Center]] (CRC). You'll often hear CRC-based control as a "Ground Control Intercept" or GCI. You can see multiple controllers, with each controlling a group of lanes.

Lanes during the NATO intervention in Kosovo; The Red tracks are the CAPs, placed strategically around the combat areas in Kosovo and the  federal republic of Yugoslavia. They're the defensive barrier between hostile territory and NATO tankers flying the yellow tracks; enemies have to go through a wall of DCA fighters first. See the "BARI" CAP in the bottom-left? It's a special type of CAP known as a "Goalie CAP", the last line of defense if something gets through the front line, or also used to backfill frontline CAPs if they have to depart station (mechanical failure or have to leave because they run out of munitions). 


![[Pasted image 20260725212937.png]]
A flight in a CAP orbit can split into two troops, one on the outbound loop, and one on the inbound loop. This gives the flight constant radar coverage of the threat axis (horizontal, on the right side) without creeping into enemy airspace. Why worry if they have a (more powerful) AWACS or CRC telling them where the enemy is? There are instances where the fighter have been able to detect enemies before the AWACS sees it, still. The most important factor in selecting CAP is optimizing threat detection; flying below intended targets lets you scan them with radar without having radar clutter from the background. Conversely, higher altitudes are more fuel efficient, meaning you can stay on station longer.   

![[Pasted image 20260725220114.png]]
Often times you have a race track orbit aligned with the threat axis, letting the aircrew maximize the amount of time they have radar pointed towards the threat on the straightaways. 

When an unauthorized aircraft enters an area where it's time for an intercept, this is called a ==Commit.== Typically the [[Joint Force Air Component Commander|JFACC]] is too busy to handle individual commits, so it can be pushed to [[Airborne Warning and Control System|AWACS]] aircraft. If needed, the authority to call a commit can be pushed all the way down to the fighters. This tiered system of authority is called [[Theater Air Control System]] (TACS). After a commit is ordered, the interceptors need to arrive at the intercept at a position that gives them an offensive advantage. They also need to be ready to deal with an adversary that decides to fire on them before they get to the intercept.
