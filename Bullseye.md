Getting help when you need it and warning allies when there's a threat  is important. "There's enemy fighters on my six" doesn't help if no one knows where you're at. The simple solution for this is to agree to common reference points ahead of time for anyone involved in the mission. Now someone could say "Enemy fighters 10 miles north of the reference point."

Using a very prominent landmark like a mountain or city makes this easier for everyone involved. During the Vietnam war, the city of Hanoi and its surrounds became a common reference point, earning the nickname of "Bullseye" among air crews. A large city on a river is easily identifiable, so when a bomber crew announced enemy interceptors were located on a bearing of "Bullseye 270/15" (Bearing of 270, 15 miles from Bullseye), this was effective.

In Desert Storm, the city of Baghdad became bullseye.

Now it's codified:
> BULLSEYE is an established reference point from which the position of an object can be referred to by bearing (magnetic) and range (nm).

There are some special rules to remember: If a contact is within 5 nm, it can be described as "at bullseye."

If you're a flight Showtime 11, you might hear a call:
> "Showtime 11, lead group, at bullseye, thirty-nine thousand, track north, hostile."

Now you and everyone know that a hostile group is flying north at 39,000 feel over the Bullseye.

Bullseye can also be given a codename (e.g. Rock) in some circumstanceS:
> "Mike, South Group, Rock 255/29, Thirty-Nine Thousand, CAP, Hostile"

Hostiles are flying a [[Combat Air Patrol]] @ 39,000 feet, at a location 29 miles ~west of Bullseye.


Note that BULLSEYE should not be truncated to "bull" to avoid it being interpreted as [[Bearing, Range, Altitude, and Aspect|BRAA]], which is another way of describing the location of a target. 

See [[Geographic Reference]]s, which are basically Bullseyes that aren't "the" Bullseye; a geographic reference point, like a mountain.
