The use of the [[Electromagnetic Spectrum]] (radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light, etc) and directed energy to control the spectrum, deny its use to adversaries, and ensure unimpeded access for friendly forces.
- The purpose is two-fold:
	- ==Deny the opponent the access to the EM Spectrum==
	- ==Ensure friendly, unimpeded access to the EM Spectrum==

Three Pillars of EW:
- ==Electronic Attack (EA)==: The offensive use of electromagnetic energy, directed energy, or anti-radiation weapons to degrade, neutralize, or destroy enemy combat capabilities
	- ==Jamming==: Broadcasting high-power signals to block or scramble enemy communications and radar.
	- ==Spoofing==: Transmitting fake signals to trick enemy navigation or drone systems into accepting incorrect coordinates.
	- Somewhat related to [[Electronic Countermeasure]]s.
- ==Electronic Protection (EP, Electronic Protective Measures)==: Defense measures taken to ensure friendly forces can still use the EM spectrum, even while the enemy is attacking it.
	- ==Frequency Hopping==: Rapidly switching radio frequencies to avoid jamming.
	- ==Hardening== electronic equipment
- ==Electronic Support (ES, Electronic Support Measures)==: The intelligence-gathering phase. It involves intercepting, identifying, and locating electromagnetic emissions to provide commanders with immediate situational awareness regarding enemy threats and locations.


> Most of the folks in the air and surface communities treat the EM spectrum as a tool, thinking "I use my radio to talk to someone, or to use my radar to go detect something", they don't think about it as an environment. Ten years later, we're seeing a reemergence of EW as a discipline in warfare, where it's become the starting point of the battlefield in Ukraine, where things start in the spectrum, then become kinetic if needed. From my experience as a submariner, dealing in the acoustic environment that we're so aware of... it was natural. If you don't have a good plan for managing the spectrum, you'll be quickly annihilated, as we saw in Ukraine.
> - Brian Clark, CSIS Podcast episode

> If you can be detected, you can be targeted. If you can be targeted, you can be engaged. If you can be engaged, you can be killed.

> If we don't put more effort into negating the ability to get targeted, we're going to find us always playing catchup in an environment that favors offensive forces. The reemergence of adaptability as a central piece of military operations; changing out software on drones every couple of days because their flight profile needs to change, changing radio frequencies or waveforms because jamming has changed on the other side, doing move-countermove competition, which has accelerated with digital technologies. Allies forces could gain an advantage because they have access to better underlying digital technologies that could allow them to adapt faster... Russia should be able to win if this were a battle of attrition, and they aren't, which is in large part driven by Ukraine's adaptability.
> Counterpoint here being our supplying of stockpiled [[M982 Excalibur]] GPS-guided artillery rounds, which worked great for a few months, then GPS jamming from the Russians made them obsolete, and it's not easy to modify these, so it will take years to get them back into service. Same thing happened with [[Guided Multiple Launch Rocket System|GMLRS]], some things happened with [[MIM-104 Patriot|PAC-3 MSE]], etc. ***You want to invest in the capacity for change and adaptation, rather than on platforms themselves.***
